# Rules: facts about Shogun 2 battles

The general always plans with these. One point per line; edit freely, the file is re-read for every battle.

- Coordinates are metres on the battle map: x grows to the east, y grows to the north. Headings are compass degrees: 0 = north, 90 = east, 180 = south, 270 = west.
- Unit sizes, missile ranges and ammunition in the report come from the game itself; trust them over general knowledge.
- Morale wins battles: units rout when they lose heart, not only when they lose men. Attacks into a unit's flank or rear, being outnumbered, heavy losses, fatigue and losing the general all lower morale.
- The general's unit inspires every unit near it. If the general dies or routs, the whole army loses a lot of morale. Never throw the general into the front line early; keep him close behind the fighting, out of missile range when possible.
- The report tags units whose morale is at risk ("morale risk MEDIUM/HIGH" with the reasons), yours and the enemy's. It is an estimate from what can be seen. Act on it: support or pull back your wavering units before they break (a routing unit spreads panic to its neighbours), and strike the enemy's shaky units, ideally in the flank or rear, to start a chain of routs.
- A routing unit is out of your control until it rallies by itself. A shattered unit will not come back. Do not give them orders.
- Formed missile infantry with ammunition (rifles, matchlocks, bows) shreds cavalry that rides at it from the front: horses are big targets and the volleys land before the charge does. Cavalry reaches missile units and artillery safely only from a flank or the rear, out of the line of fire of the units that guard them.
- Crossing open ground under rifle and artillery fire costs heavily: the losses before contact can decide the battle. Rifles (late-era infantry) reach about as far as bows and fire much faster; artillery reaches across most of the field.
- Spear infantry (yari, naginata, pikes) defeats cavalry that charges its front; cavalry should not charge spears head-on.
- Cavalry is fast and hits hard on the charge, especially into the flank or rear of infantry that is already fighting, and against missile units. In a long frontal melee it loses.
- Sword infantry (katana, no-dachi, warrior monks) is strong in melee against other infantry but suffers against cavalry charges unless supported by spears.
- Missile units (bows, matchlocks, rifles, artillery) are weak in melee. They shoot only within their range and only while they have ammunition. Matchlocks and rifles cannot fire over friendly units in front of them; bows can arc over them.
- Running tires units; tired units fight worse and break sooner. Walk over long distances, run for the last stretch or to catch a moving target.
- Units only move along the ground: they cannot cross impassable terrain, and in forests they are slower and harder to shoot.
- High ground matters: units fighting downhill hit harder and keep their morale better, missile units shoot further from higher ground, and climbing a slope tires units. Prefer to receive an attack on higher ground and avoid charging uphill. The report's TERRAIN table and each unit's "alt" show heights; the table only knows ground where units have stood, so a slope beyond them is unknown.
- Sieges: the report lists the FORTIFICATIONS with ids (b12): gates, towers, the keep and wall sections, who holds each, and how many men each can hold. Altitude ("alt") tells who is up on the castle level and who is below.
- Defenders: missile units garrisoned on walls and towers (task garrison) shoot down at attackers and are hard to reach; keep melee units behind the walls at the gates and where ladders come up, and a reserve near the keep.
- Attackers: infantry can scale wall sections (task climb) or break a gate (attack_building); cavalry is poor at both and better kept for the open ground or for after a breach. Taking and holding the keep's area wins a castle (task occupy).
- Your orders take a few seconds to reach the battlefield, and the battle goes on while you think: plan for where things will be, not only where they are.
